# Comprehensive Survey on Video and Multimodal Generation: Architectures, Temporal Consistency, Scaling Laws, and Evaluation Frameworks

## TL;DR
- **Diffusion Transformer (DiT) Paradigm Dominance:** Modern video generation foundation models have largely shifted from U-Net backbones to Diffusion Transformer (DiT) architectures (such as Sora, W.A.L.T, CogVideoX, and Latte), leveraging self-attention mechanisms over spacetime latent patches to model complex spatiotemporal dependencies and improve generation stability [1][2][3][4].
- **Unified Multimodal and Interactive Generation:** Recent frameworks bridge language, audio, and video under unified large multimodal models (e.g., JavisGPT for joint sounding-video generation and GameGen-X for interactive open-world game video generation) using specialized synchrony-aware learnable queries and instruction-tuning [5][6].
- **Precise Scaling Laws in Diffusion Models:** Pretraining loss and scaling behavior follow power-law relationships with training compute budgets ($C$). Image-domain Diffusion Transformers establish specific parameter and token allocation exponents ($N_{\text{opt}} \propto C^{0.5681}$, $D_{\text{opt}} \propto C^{0.4319}$) alongside FID scaling [7], whereas video diffusion transformers incorporate distinct token-cost relations that account for temporal context length [8].
- **Multi-Dimensional Evaluation Suites:** Traditional fragmented metrics (PSNR, SSIM, FVD) are being superseded by rigorous, disentangled benchmark suites like VBench++ (evaluating 16 hierarchical dimensions) [9], UniVBench [10], and large-scale human-rated frameworks such as AIGVE-60K (encompassing 58,500 videos across 30 models and 2.6 million human ratings cited from comprehensive evaluation surveys) [4].
- **Computational Efficiency Paradigms:** To mitigate heavy quadratic attention costs and long generation latencies, acceleration strategies combine step distillation, efficient attention, model quantization (QAT/PTQ with timestep-aware calibration), and training-free spectral attention blending (e.g., FreeLong) [11][12][13].

## Background
The field of video and multimodal generation has undergone a profound transformation, evolving from early generative adversarial networks (GANs) and variational autoencoders (VAEs) to denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPMs), autoregressive (AR) transformers, and hybrid Diffusion-Transformer (DiT) architectures [1][14]. Early video generation methods struggled with temporal flickering, ghosting artifacts, and limited temporal horizons due to constraints in modeling high-dimensional pixel spaces [4]. The introduction of Latent Diffusion Models (LDMs) revolutionized visual synthesis by compressing high-resolution pixel data into lower-dimensional spatial-temporal latent spaces via 3D VQ-VAE or causal encoder networks, significantly reducing computational overhead [1][2][14].

Concurrently, the emergence of Vision-Language Foundation Models (VLFMs) such as CLIP, BLIP-2, and Flamingo established robust cross-modal alignment foundations, enabling text-conditional video generation models to interpret complex natural language prompts [1]. More recently, the field has expanded toward unified multimodal generation, where models jointly handle video comprehension, text-to-video synthesis, image-to-video animation, audio-visual synchrony, and interactive instruction-guided generation [5][6]. Despite these breakthroughs, video generation remains computationally intensive, requiring advanced spatiotemporal attention mechanisms, careful scaling laws, and multidimensional benchmark suites to measure true perceptual and semantic fidelity.

## Video Generation Foundation Models and Architectural Paradigms
The architectural landscape of video generation foundation models is divided between diffusion-based frameworks and autoregressive (AR) models [15]. Diffusion models build upon foundational probabilistic formulations including Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs), Score-based Generative Models (SGMs), and Stochastic Differential Equations (Score SDEs) [2]. While early video diffusion models relied on U-Net backbones with inflated 3D convolutions, state-of-the-art systems have embraced Diffusion Transformer (DiT) architectures [2][15].

DiT models such as Sora, W.A.L.T, and CogVideoX replace traditional U-Net convolutions with self-attention operations over spacetime patches of video and image latent codes [2][3][4]. For instance, W.A.L.T utilizes a causal encoder to compress images and videos into a unified latent space, employing window attention tailored for joint spatial and spatiotemporal generative modeling alongside cascading super-resolution networks [2]. Sora trains text-conditional diffusion models jointly on videos and images of variable durations, resolutions, and aspect ratios by decomposing raw compressed videos into sequences of spacetime patches that act as transformer tokens [3].

Complementing pure diffusion models, autoregressive (AR) video generation models process inputs sequentially and leverage in-context learning to handle multiple modalities under a unified structure [15]. Hybrid designs merge VQ-VAE latent quantization with GPT-style autoregressive transformers [14]. For example, VideoGPT implements a hybrid architecture utilizing 3D convolutions in a VQ-VAE framework to extract deep spatial-temporal features, while frameworks like NOVA introduce non-quantized autoregressive video generation supporting high-efficiency causal frame prediction combined with block-wise causal masking attention within frames [15][14].

## Spatiotemporal Consistency and Long Video Generation
A central challenge in video generation is maintaining spatiotemporal consistency—ensuring that spatial semantic attributes (subject identity, scene layout, and lighting) remain stable while temporal transitions between adjacent frames evolve smoothly according to conditional distributions $p(x_t | x_{<t}, C)$ [16].

To handle long video generation without exploding computational complexity, research has branched into two primary paradigms: the Divide and Conquer approach and the Temporal Autoregressive paradigm [17]. Temporally Consistent Transformers such as TECO address quadratic attention complexity by aggressively downsampling in space before feeding representations into a temporal transformer, subsequently expanding back out with a spatial MaskGit applied per frame [11]. TECO conditions its encoder $z_t = E(x_t, x_{n-1})$ on previous frames via channel-wise concatenation to capture relative motion dynamics and optimize vector-quantized codebook utilization [11].

Time-space decoupling strategies—such as OmniTokenizer, SweetTok, and FlashVideo—integrate window attention and causal attention to decompose videos into compact spatiotemporal components [16][4]. For training-free long video generation, techniques like FreeLong introduce SpectralBlend Temporal Attention (SpectralBlend-TA) to extend short-video diffusion models to long horizons without requiring full network retraining [12]. Furthermore, frame interpolation techniques (such as optical flow estimation and deep learning methods like IFNet) enhance temporal continuity and frame rates by synthesizing intermediate frames between adjacent outputs [16].

## Multimodal and Interactive Generation Frameworks
As generative models mature beyond text-to-video (T2V) and image-to-video (I2V) tasks, research has shifted toward unified multi-modal architectures capable of joint comprehension and generation across text, images, audio, and interactive controls [5][6].

JavisGPT exemplifies this shift as a unified multimodal large language model (MLLM) designed for Joint Audio-Video (JAV) comprehension and generation [5]. JavisGPT employs a concise encoder-LLM-decoder architecture featuring a SyncFusion module for spatio-temporal audio-video fusion and synchrony-aware learnable queries that bridge a pretrained JAV-DiT generator, enabling temporally coherent sounding-video generation from multimodal instructions through a three-stage training pipeline (multimodal pretraining, audio-video fine-tuning, and large-scale instruction tuning) [5].

In the interactive domain, GameGen-X demonstrates open-world game video generation and interactive control through a two-stage training process comprising text-to-video pretraining followed by instruction tuning with InstructNet [6]. Similarly, fine-grained evaluation frameworks such as OmniCapBench reframe audio-visual captioning as a deep-structured diagnostic benchmark to expose capability limits in continuous audio-visual reasoning and cross-modal alignment [18].

## Scaling Laws and Compute Allocation in Diffusion Models
Understanding the relationship between compute budgets, model capacity, and data volume is critical for efficient diffusion model development. Research into scaling laws for Diffusion Transformers distinguishes between image-generation DiT findings and video-specific DiT formulations.

In image-generation DiT scaling studies, pretraining loss follows a power-law relationship with training compute ($C$), formalized across budgets ranging from $1\text{e}17$ to $6\text{e}18$ FLOPs [7]. The foundational relationship between compute, tokens, and model parameters is modeled as $C = 6ND$, reflecting that processing a single token requires approximately $6N$ FLOPs across forward and backward passes [7]. In this image DiT setting, empirical investigations into optimal parameter allocation ($N_{\text{opt}}$) and optimal token count ($D_{\text{opt}}$) demonstrate power-law scaling functions of compute budget $C$ where $N_{\text{opt}} \propto C^{0.5681}$ and $D_{\text{opt}} \propto C^{0.4319}$, with generation quality metrics such as Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) following corresponding power-law trends [7].

By contrast, video diffusion transformers operate under distinct token-cost dynamics that explicitly incorporate temporal context length and frame dimensions into the token-cost relation [8]. Rather than generalizing image-generation exponents directly to video, video-specific DiT scaling analyses account for spatiotemporal token sequences and context length dependencies to properly evaluate optimal model and data sizing under video training constraints [8].

## Evaluation Frameworks, Benchmarks, and Trustworthiness
Evaluating video generation models has historically suffered from fragmented metrics. Traditional full-reference metrics like PSNR and SSIM fail to capture dynamic video artifacts such as ghosting and temporal flickering, prompting motion-weighted adaptations like PSNRDIV and Swin Transformer-based evaluators like VFIPS [4].

To address these limitations, comprehensive benchmark suites have emerged. VBench++ dissects video generation quality into hierarchical, disentangled dimensions separating coarse-level Video Quality and Video-Condition Consistency across 16 specific dimensions (including subject identity inconsistency, motion smoothness, temporal flickering, and spatial relationships) while incorporating robust Image-to-Video (I2V) evaluation suites [9]. Large-scale human-rated datasets like AIGVE-60K (introduced in the original LOVE paper) comprise 58,500 videos from 30 T2V models, while the figure of 2.6 million human ratings appears in the comprehensive CVPR evaluation survey [4]. These frameworks introduce advanced metrics like LOVE (via Large Multimodal Models) to disentangle perceptual quality, text-video correspondence, and task-specific accuracy [4].

At the intersection of general foundation models, UniVBench provides a unified evaluation framework across four core tasks—video understanding, video generation, video editing, and video reconstruction—using multi-shot copyright-free videos and standardized agentic evaluation systems (UniV-Eval) to measure progress toward general-purpose instruction-following video intelligence [10].

## Efficiency, Quantization, and Acceleration
The heavy computational burden of video diffusion models—where sampling times can exceed 90 seconds to 2 minutes per video—has catalyzed extensive research into efficiency optimizations [9][13]. Accelerated video diffusion methods focus on four primary pillars: step distillation (reducing Denoiser Function Evaluations / NFE), efficient attention (sparsifying or linearizing expensive attention operations), model compression (quantization and pruning), and cache/trajectory optimization [13].

Quantization approaches for video diffusion encompass Quantization-Aware Training (QAT) and Post-Training Quantization (PTQ), utilizing adaptive bit-widths ($W_{b_w}A_{b_a}$) and timestep-aware calibration (e.g., TaQ-DiT, DVD-Quant, Q-DiT, ViDiT-Q) to handle fluctuating diffusion statistics across timesteps $t$ [13]. To mitigate quantization error, advanced frameworks employ quantization bias compensation—such as QuantSparse and FPSAttention within attention modules, Q-VDiT in parameter/feature space, and S2Q-VDiT using data- and token-level compensation [13]. Hardware-dependent metrics (throughput, latency, peak VRAM) and hardware-agnostic measures (NFE, FLOPs) are combined with perceptual suites like VBench, DOVER, and FasterVQA to holistically evaluate efficiency gains [13].

## Trends and Open Problems
- **Unified Omni-Modal Foundation Models:** Moving beyond isolated text-to-video generation toward native omni-modal architectures that seamlessly interleave text, image, audio, and video generation and comprehension in real-time [5].
- **Interactive and Simulation Capabilities:** Enhancing video generation models to act as interactive world simulators capable of responding to user controls, game physics, and multi-turn instruction tuning [3][6].
- **Scalable Training and Data Efficiency:** Leveraging precise scaling laws and synthetic data pipelines to optimize compute-optimal token and parameter allocations for billion-parameter DiTs [8][7].
- **Standardized Multi-Task Evaluation:** Widespread adoption of agentic evaluation suites (like UniVBench and VBench++) that unify perception, generation, and editing diagnostics to replace ad-hoc metrics [9][10].
- **Real-Time Deployment via Quantization and Distillation:** Overcoming hardware latency bottlenecks through advanced timestep-aware quantization, step distillation, and training-free attention blending [12][13].
